Design philosophy

Make the
system legible.

What I believe design is for, how I work, and the tools I've kept or thrown out along the way.

Most software fails people not because it lacks power, but because it never explains itself.

What I believe

For a year I explained technical systems to people who had not asked to learn them. Someone would arrive with a device that had stopped doing the one thing they needed, and the job was to turn a cause they could not see into an action they could take. I came to think that is the whole of design, and that an interface is just that same conversation, held in advance, at scale.

So my first value is legibility over elegance. A system should tell you what state it is in, what it just did, and what will happen next. When SmartEnroll tells a student they are ineligible for a course, the design failure would be stopping there. The useful thing is naming the prerequisite they are missing and what unlocks it. We built the recursive prerequisite relation into the schema specifically so the interface could answer "why not?" instead of just "no."

My second value is that the hard part is usually underneath. It is tempting to treat design as the layer you apply at the end. But the reason Hobby+ could show a student their credit balance in one glance is that the data model made credits a first-class entity rather than a derived number. Interface problems are very often data problems wearing a costume.

My third value is designing for the person having a bad day. Empty states, errors, and edge cases are where people actually need help, and they are the first thing a deadline eats. I would rather ship four screens with honest failure states than nine that only work when everything goes right.

How I work

I start by writing the problem as a sentence from the user's side, not the system's. For SmartEnroll that sentence was: "I don't know what I'm allowed to take next term, and finding out means opening five tabs." Every later decision got measured against it. If a feature did not shorten that sentence, it did not go in.

Then I model before I draw. I sketch entities and relationships on paper first, because the shape of the data determines what the interface can honestly promise. On SmartEnroll our first ER diagram treated requirements as a flat list; it could not express "this course satisfies either of these two requirements," which meant the degree-progress screen would have lied. Revising the model fixed a UI problem we had not yet built.

I build low fidelity and ugly on purpose. Early mockups in grayscale with real text, never lorem ipsum, because fake content hides the places where the writing has to do the work. Then I put it in front of someone outside the team. Our Hobby+ onboarding flow was three steps long because the first person we tested with asked, out loud, "wait, am I done?"

Finally I iterate against something measurable. This came from DeepRacer more than from any design course: I tuned a reinforcement learning model lap after lap, and the only thing that mattered was whether the car held the line. It taught me to define what "better" means before changing anything, which I now do with interfaces too.

Tools I've adopted

  • Paper and a pen, first. Nothing lets me reject ten ideas as fast. The cost of throwing out a sketch is zero, which is exactly why I make more of them.
  • ER diagrams as a design tool, not a deliverable. I now draw the data model before any screen. It has caught interface lies twice.
  • Figma for structure, code for truth. I use Figma to agree on layout and hierarchy with a team, then move to real HTML and CSS early, because a static mockup cannot tell you how a page feels at 320px or on a slow connection.
  • Kanban over sprints, for small teams. On four-to-five person student teams, a visible board of what is actually in flight beat ceremony we would not keep up.
  • Writing the copy before the component. If I cannot write the error message, I do not understand the state yet.

Tools I've rejected

  • High-fidelity mockups as a starting point. I used to open Figma first and design in colour immediately. It made me defensive about work I had already polished, and it hid structural problems behind nice type. Grayscale first, colour last.
  • Component libraries chosen before the problem. Reaching for a UI kit on day one quietly decides your layout, spacing, and interaction vocabulary for you. This portfolio is hand-written HTML, CSS, and JavaScript for exactly that reason. I wanted the type scale and the motion to be arguments I had to make, not defaults I inherited.
  • Lorem ipsum. Placeholder text makes every layout look like it works. Real content is where designs break, so I use it from the first sketch.
  • Animating everything. My instinct used to be to fade and slide every section into view. I have cut it back to one orchestrated moment on arrival and motion that responds to something you did. Movement that nobody asked for is noise, and it makes a page feel slower than it is.
  • Designing only the happy path. The honest reason I rejected this is that I shipped it once, and watched a user hit a blank screen with no explanation. Empty and error states are now part of the first pass.

What I'm still working on

Accessibility is the area where I know the most about what I do not yet do well. I now check colour contrast and keyboard focus as a matter of course, but I have not yet tested anything I have built with a screen reader user, and I am aware that auditing for compliance is not the same as designing with someone. That is the next thing I want to get right, and it is the gap I would most like to close before I graduate.
